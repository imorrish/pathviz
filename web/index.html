How PathViz works

In plain words

A packet crosses a fixed chain of checkpoints on its way through the Linux network stack. PathViz puts an observer at every checkpoint. An observer only notes which packets passed by and never touches them.

PathViz collects the notes, finds the ones that belong to the same packet, and lights up the checkpoints this packet passed. A packet that reached a checkpoint and was not seen at the next one was stopped in between.

The layers

  • Device (L1): the ingress hook of every network device, right after the device received a frame, and the egress hook, right before the device sends one.
  • Bridge (L2): the five netfilter hooks of a Linux bridge. They show which ports got a copy of a frame, and why: the bridge looked the destination up in its MAC table or flooded the frame.
  • IP (L3): the five netfilter hooks of the IP layer and the stages inside them (raw, mangle, nat, filter), where iptables and nftables rules run.

Under the hood

  1. PathViz installs one nftables table per layer, all named pathviz: in the netdev, bridge, and inet families, in the network namespace you watch.
  2. Every table has probe chains at the start and at the end of each hook. The IP table also has one right before every stage.
  3. A probe logs the packets that match the filter to an NFLOG group and lets them go on unchanged.
  4. The first IP probe that sees a matching packet remembers its identity (the IP ID, the TCP sequence numbers, and similar fields) for half a second. The later IP probes recognize the packet by it, even after NAT rewrote its addresses or ports. The device and bridge probes see a packet with the addresses it has at that point.
  5. PathViz reads the group and puts the records of one packet together. The names of the devices, the bridges' MAC tables, and what is at the other end of a veth pair (a container, a pod, a namespace) come from ip, bridge, and the container runtimes, once a second.
